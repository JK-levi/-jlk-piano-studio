Weekly Keyboard Practice (20–30 minutes)
Theme: Finding an idea and letting it evolve
The goal this week isn't to learn a song—it's to discover a short musical phrase that could become your piece.

1. Black-Key Exploration (5 minutes)
Set a simple metronome or beat-only track around 70–80 BPM.

Using only the black keys, spend a few minutes improvising without worrying about harmony. Since the black keys form a pentatonic scale, almost everything will sound cohesive.

Try:

Short 3–5 note ideas.
Lots of silence between phrases.
Repeating one motif, changing only the rhythm.
Don't chase complexity—look for one phrase you genuinely like.

2. New Chord Voicings (7 minutes)
Keep your right hand improvising on the black keys while your left hand experiments with these simple voicings:

B♭sus2 → (B♭–C–F)
G♭maj7(no3) → (G♭–D♭–F)
D♭add9 → (D♭–E♭–A♭)
Don't think about the names while playing. Listen for:

Which chord feels like "home."
Which chord creates tension.
Which transition surprises you.
Hold each chord for several measures before changing.

3. Split Voice Experiment (5–7 minutes)
On your Roland GO:KEYS 3:

Left: Warm pad or soft strings.
Right: Felt piano or mellow electric piano.
Play sustained chords with the pad while letting the piano speak in short melodic phrases.

Focus on dynamics:

Let the pad stay almost motionless.
Allow the piano to become the storyteller.
4. Build Your Own Piece (8–10 minutes)
Keep the beat-only track running.

Challenge yourself to create:

An A section (about 20–30 seconds).
Repeat it.
Change only one thing:
rhythm,
ending,
bass note,
or one chord.
That's the beginning of a B section.

Record the whole improvisation on your keyboard or phone. Don't stop if you make mistakes—sometimes the best ideas happen when you're trying to recover.

Listening Inspiration (Apple Music)
Retrograde — James Blake
Listen for:

Space between phrases.
Minimal harmony.
How repetition slowly evolves into something emotional.
The way texture changes without becoming busy.
Experience — Ludovico Einaudi
Listen for:

Building intensity through repetition rather than complexity.
Small harmonic shifts creating a large emotional impact.
The patience of the arrangement—nothing feels rushed.
This week's creative challenge
By the end of the session, try to have one 20–30 second idea that you would actually want to hear again tomorrow. Don't judge whether it's "finished." Your only goal is to capture a musical seed worth revisiting.
